# Visual narration

Read this before designing any content slide. It is the most important part of the skill.

### Visual narrative and spatial thinking

Design each slide as a **visual sentence**. The title states the claim. The composition makes the claim perceptible. The evidence earns belief.

Before drawing, write three lines:

- **Claim:** What should the audience conclude?
- **Evidence:** What must they see to believe it?
- **Response:** What should they decide, remember, or question?

If these cannot be stated plainly, the slide is not ready to design.

### 1. Identify the visual verb

Ask what the evidence is doing. Select the dominant relationship—not a familiar layout.

- **Compare:** reveal a meaningful difference.
- **Change:** show movement over time or between states.
- **Sequence:** show order, progression, or gates.
- **Locate:** show position against dimensions, thresholds, or peers.
- **Decompose:** show what contributes to a whole or outcome.
- **Contain:** show scope, inclusion, ownership, or layers.
- **Connect:** show dependency, exchange, influence, or feedback.
- **Converge:** show several inputs producing one outcome.
- **Diverge:** show one source creating alternatives or consequences.
- **Prioritise:** distinguish what matters now from what can wait.
- **Expose uncertainty:** show a range, confidence, sensitivity, or missing evidence.

Most slides need one dominant verb. If two verbs are equally important, split the slide or establish a clear primary and secondary reading.

### 2. Choose the strongest visual evidence

Prefer the most direct representation that preserves truth:

1. The actual object, customer moment, product screen, place, or process.
2. A chart using the relevant quantities.
3. A diagram encoding the real relationship.
4. A short structured statement when no meaningful visual evidence exists.

Do not convert prose into icons or diagrams merely to make it look visual. A sentence is better than a false picture.

### 3. Compose around one focal claim

Decide what the eye should register in the first three seconds. Give it dominant position, scale, contrast, or isolation. Then arrange supporting evidence so the eye can verify the claim without searching.

Use hierarchy deliberately:

- **Position** establishes reading order.
- **Scale** signals importance or magnitude.
- **Proximity** signals relationship.
- **Alignment** signals equivalence or continuity.
- **Containment** signals membership or boundary.
- **Contrast** signals distinction or exception.
- **Direction** signals sequence, flow, or causality.
- **Repetition** signals comparability.
- **Whitespace** isolates meaning; it is not empty decoration.

Use no more than two strong hierarchy devices at once. When position, scale, colour, shape, and arrows all compete, none leads.

### 4. Build a reading path

A good slide has an entry, a path, and a landing point.

- **Entry:** the focal claim, image, number, or anomaly.
- **Path:** the minimum evidence needed to understand why it is true.
- **Landing:** the implication, decision, or unresolved tension.

Test the slide at thumbnail size. The intended order should remain visible before the body text is readable.

### 5. Preserve semantic honesty

Use spatial relationships only when they are true.

- Near means related.
- Aligned means comparable or continuous.
- Inside means contained or owned.
- Larger means more important or quantitatively larger; make the meaning clear.
- An arrow means direction, transfer, influence, or causality—not “look here.”
- A closed loop means recurrence or feedback—not generic completeness.
- Symmetry implies equivalence or balance.
- A centre implies primacy, coordination, or attraction.

Do not use a neat diagram if it asserts a relationship the evidence does not support.

### 6. Edit visually, not only verbally

Run four removal passes:

1. **Frame removal:** remove boxes, backgrounds, rules, and labels that do not encode meaning.
2. **Text compression:** replace paragraphs with a claim, direct labels, and short annotations.
3. **Evidence reduction:** retain only evidence needed to support the title claim.
4. **Style reduction:** remove decorative colour, icons, shadows, and connectors.

Create space by removing weak material. Do not shrink everything to preserve it.

### 7. Use repeated containers only when repetition is the point

Cards are valid for genuinely comparable peers, selectable options, modular components, or repeated records. They are not a default unit of thought.

When cards are justified:

- use a shared internal structure;
- vary emphasis only when the data warrants it;
- order them by a meaningful dimension;
- keep one idea per card;
- avoid placing a second card grid inside a card.

Tables remain correct for reference and exact lookup. Lists remain correct for ordered or grouped language. Not every slide needs a diagram.

## Reusable spatial structures

Treat these as **structures of meaning**, not templates. The same structure can look radically different across decks.

| Audience must perceive | Reusable structure | Use when | Avoid when |
|---|---|---|---|
| One difference matters | **Dominant contrast** | Two states, choices, segments, or outcomes differ on one decisive dimension | Several dimensions need exact comparison; use a table or small multiples |
| A result changed over time | **Annotated trajectory** | Direction, inflection, volatility, or before/after movement matters | Only the endpoints matter; use a delta or paired state |
| A transition has stages | **Open sequence** | Order, maturity, workflow, or gates are real | Stages repeat or interact; use a loop or dependency map |
| Position determines action | **Threshold field** | Portfolio choices, risk/return, urgency/impact, or performance bands | Axes are vague labels with no defensible scale |
| A few factors explain an outcome | **Contribution build** | Drivers add to, subtract from, or account for a result | Contributions overlap or cannot be quantified |
| One whole contains layers | **Nested zones** | Scope, governance, system layers, or inclusion is the claim | The layers interact more than they contain one another |
| Many inputs produce one outcome | **Convergence** | Capabilities, signals, or workstreams combine into a decision or result | Inputs are merely a list and have no integration mechanism |
| One cause creates several effects | **Divergence** | Scenarios, consequences, channels, or downstream impacts matter | Branches rejoin or depend on each other; show the network |
| Relationships radiate from a centre | **Hub and spokes** | A true platform, coordinator, shared service, or central actor exists | Peer-to-peer links dominate or the centre is only decorative |
| Value moves between actors | **Flow map** | Information, money, ownership, materials, or customer hand-offs move | Only affiliation matters; use proximity or containment |
| A system reinforces or balances itself | **Feedback loop** | Recurrence and feedback are essential to the claim | The process is actually linear or the loop cannot be explained |
| Several cases share one test | **Small multiples** | Repeated comparison across regions, products, periods, or scenarios | Each case uses different measures or scales without clear disclosure |
| One option deserves attention | **Ranked field** | Prioritisation, concentration, or a long tail is the point | Exact values across many attributes matter; use a table |
| The future is not a point estimate | **Uncertainty band or scenario fan** | Range, sensitivity, probability, or confidence matters | Scenarios are categorical and not ordered |
| A gap blocks the desired state | **Bridge or tension** | Current state, obstacle, intervention, and destination form the argument | The path is speculative or multiple paths matter equally |
| An idea becomes concrete through an example | **Hero object with callouts** | A product screen, customer moment, document, place, or artifact is primary evidence | The object is generic stock imagery rather than evidence |

### Useful combinations

Combine structures only when one is clearly subordinate:

- **Trajectory + callout:** show the trend, then isolate the inflection that matters.
- **Threshold field + highlight:** show the portfolio, then spotlight the decision candidate.
- **Hero object + flow:** show the artifact, then trace the few interactions around it.
- **Before/after + bridge:** show the change and the mechanism that produces it.
- **Small multiples + ranked conclusion:** show repeated evidence, then identify the pattern.

Avoid “diagram soup”: sequence + nested circles + arrows + icons + cards usually encodes several incompatible metaphors.

## Scenario examples

### “Three capabilities will reduce claims leakage”

**Weak:** three equal cards with icons and descriptions.

**Stronger:** a leakage funnel as the main evidence. Place each capability at the point where it prevents loss. End with the retained-value outcome. This shows mechanism, not merely membership.

### “Europe is the growth constraint despite strong global performance”

**Weak:** four regional KPI cards.

**Stronger:** small-multiple regional trajectories with Europe visually isolated. Annotate the divergence point. Put the global aggregate in a quiet reference position.

### “The transformation has five workstreams”

**Weak:** five coloured pillars.

**Stronger:** first decide what the claim is. If timing matters, use a sequence. If coordination matters, use a convergence structure. If ownership matters, use bounded swim lanes. If they are merely a list, use a disciplined list and do not fabricate a diagram.

### “We should fund option B”

**Weak:** three equally weighted option cards.

**Stronger:** plot the options on the two decision dimensions that actually govern funding. Highlight B and annotate the decisive evidence and trade-off. Keep secondary criteria in a compact table or appendix.

### “The operating model connects local expertise with a shared platform”

**Weak:** a central circle surrounded by teams because hub-and-spoke looks strategic.

**Stronger:** use a hub only if work or authority genuinely passes through it. Otherwise show shared platform capabilities as a horizontal substrate beneath local teams, with only the actual exchanges connected.

### “AI changes the employee workflow”

**Weak:** a row of process boxes with AI stars added to each.

**Stronger:** show the current workflow quietly, then make the changed decision points dominant. Use callouts for what is automated, augmented, or still human-owned. The change—not the process inventory—is the story.

## A compact design test

Before finalising each slide, ask:

1. Can someone state the claim after three seconds?
2. Does the composition make the key relationship visible without reading every word?
3. Is the primary evidence stronger than the decoration?
4. Does every spatial cue mean what it appears to mean?
5. Can one frame, label, colour, icon, or connector be removed?
6. Is the implication clear?
7. Would a sentence, chart, or photograph be more honest than the diagram?

If the answer to 1, 2, 4, or 6 is no, redesign the slide. If the answer to 5 is yes, keep editing.
